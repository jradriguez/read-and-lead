# Learning science and experience research

Research date: 2026-09-12. Decision support for the
[product excellence roadmap](product-excellence-roadmap.md), not approval of the
prototype's teaching material or a claim that it improves reading.

## Recommendation and research scope

Build explicit, cumulative reading instruction inside a purposeful robot workshop.
Use Montessori-inspired orderly materials, demonstrations, bounded choice and
independence; use STEM-style prediction, making and testing as meaningful context.
Keep oral language, understanding and connected reading alongside word decoding.
Animation should clarify an action or meaning and support the learner's agency.

This is a targeted evidence review, not an exhaustive systematic review of every
teaching philosophy or every app. Parallel research covered foundational literacy,
Montessori/guided play/Waldorf comparisons, educational-app outcomes, child HCI,
competitors and native performance. Sources were selected for relevance to beginning
English readers, methodological detail, independent evaluation and actionable
limitations. The review deliberately includes null findings and critiques.

Priority order: primary studies and systematic reviews; government evidence reviews;
authoritative platform documentation; official product descriptions for observable
features. A publisher's claim, an award, a store rating and a controlled learning
result answer different questions. None ranks this prototype against the market.
No competitor app was installed or played during this research.

## Evidence register

Confidence below concerns the stated finding in its studied setting. It does not
transfer automatically to this app. Standardized effects such as `g = 0.24` are
not percentages, months of learning or predicted gains for Read to Lead.

| ID / source | Design and finding | Limits and decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| L01 — Foorman et al., *Foundational Skills to Support Reading for Understanding in Kindergarten Through 3rd Grade* (2016; revised 2019), [IES/WWC](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/21/Published) | Evidence-reviewed K–3 practice guide. Strong evidence for linking sound awareness to letters and teaching decoding/word analysis; moderate for connected reading. | Strong basis for the instructional direction, primarily from school instruction. Explicit modeling, blending/segmenting, word building and early cumulative connected text belong in the app. The guide's academic-language recommendation has a minimal evidence rating in its framework; that does not mean language is unimportant. |
| L02 — Castles, Rastle & Nation, *Ending the Reading Wars: Reading Acquisition From Novice to Expert* (2018), [original review](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1529100618772271) | Integrative review explains alphabetic decoding, fluent word recognition, language and comprehension. Does not establish that synthetic phonics uniquely outperforms analytic phonics. | Strong broad mechanism evidence; not a trial of one app or branded sequence. Use systematic instruction and meaningful print experience. Avoid selling one precise sequence as the uniquely proven answer. |
| L03 — West et al., *Early language screening and intervention can be delivered successfully at scale* (2021), [Oxford paper record](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A5d4ea2cb-5201-40d7-b9e9-d4720b3d0d69) | Cluster RCT in 193 English schools: 5,879 screened, 1,173 eligible. Twenty-week NELI improved a standardized language composite, `d = 0.26`, versus usual provision. | Supported, trained-adult intervention; the screening app was not the treatment. Some authors contributed to the programme/training. Teach meanings orally and encourage adult conversation, but do not claim narration reproduces this intervention. |
| L04 — Skene et al., *Can guidance during play enhance children's learning and development in educational contexts?* (2022), [systematic review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cdev.13730) | 39 studies reviewed; 17 meta-analyzed, N = 3,893. Guided play benefited some mathematics/spatial/executive-function outcomes. Early literacy versus direct instruction: `g = 0.28`, 95% CI −0.22 to 0.79, only two studies/n = 233. | No clear literacy advantage; heterogeneous interventions and substantial bias concerns. Adult guidance is not the same as scripted software. Use guided play for purposeful practice and choice, with explicit phonics instruction. |
| L05 — Randolph et al., *Montessori education's impact on academic and nonacademic outcomes: A systematic review* (2023), [Campbell review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/cl2.1330) | 32 studies from preschool to secondary, searched through February 2020. Academic composite `g = 0.24`; language `0.17`; nonacademic composite `0.33`. | Promising whole-school evidence with varied implementations and publication-bias concerns; disclosed Montessori connections among reviewers. Its data-point count is not a unique-child sample size. Borrow design principles as hypotheses; no Montessori-equivalent app outcome follows. |
| L06 — Lillard et al., *A national randomized controlled trial of the impact of public Montessori preschool at the end of kindergarten* (2025), [study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12582262/), [WWC review, January 2026](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/95226) | Admission-lottery study at 24 US public Montessori schools; 588 consented children. The study reports advantages in several outcomes. | WWC meets standards **with reservations** because of high attrition, with analytic baseline equivalence. Promising school evidence; selected consent sample and crossover limit inference. It cannot identify which isolated Montessori feature would help a touchscreen app. |
| L07 — Suggate, Schaughency & Reese, *Children learning to read later catch up to children reading earlier* (2013), [university record](https://pure.york.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/children-learning-to-read-later-catch-up-to-children-reading-earl/), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecresq.2012.04.004) | Two nonexperimental New Zealand comparisons of earlier/later instruction in state/Steiner contexts, n = 287 and 83 additional children. Early advantages disappeared around age 11; later starters showed better comprehension in the second sample. | Low causal certainty: timing, school/family selection and wider curriculum cannot be separated fully. Respect readiness and avoid age competition; this does not prove that delaying instruction causes benefits or justify withholding it from a ready learner. |
| L08 — Kim et al., *Measures Matter: A Meta-Analysis of the Effects of Educational Apps on Preschool to Grade 3 Children's Literacy and Math Skills* (2021), [author-institution paper](https://cepr.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum9881/files/2026-01/kim-et-al-2021-measures-matter-a-meta-analysis-of-the-effects-of-educational-apps-on-preschool-to-grade-3-children-s.pdf) | 36 experimental/quasi-experimental studies, 285 effects. Overall `g = 0.31` (95% CI 0.20–0.42); literacy `0.35` (0.13–0.57). Effects were larger on researcher-created measures and constrained skills. | Heterogeneous apps, contexts and support. Some apps help targeted skills; broad transfer is less certain. Test fresh combinations and independent outcomes rather than only familiar game items. These pooled effects are not a forecast. |
| L09 — Worth et al., *GraphoGame Rime: Evaluation report and executive summary* (2018), [independent NFER/EEF report](https://www.nfer.ac.uk/media/idlhbicn/graphogame_rime_evaluation_report_and_executive_summary.pdf) | Randomized trial in 15 English schools, low-phonics Year 2 pupils: 401 randomized, 362 in primary analysis. Reading `g = −0.06`, **97%** CI −0.23 to 0.12 versus usual literacy support. Staff/pupils reported engagement. | No demonstrated superiority in this setting; neither equivalence proof nor a finding that all reading apps fail. The unusual confidence level follows the trial design. A strong reason to compare learning with credible support rather than treat enjoyment as efficacy. |
| L10 — Takacs, Swart & Bus, *Benefits and Pitfalls of Multimedia and Interactive Features in Technology-Enhanced Storybooks* (2015), [meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4647204/) | 43 studies, 2,147 children ages 3–10. Small benefits for story comprehension (`g = 0.17`) and expressive vocabulary (`0.20`); story-relevant multimedia helped, while interactive extras could distract. | Mostly story/listening outcomes, older heterogeneous technologies; not independent decoding evidence. Put semantic animations after attempts and keep unrelated hotspots quiet during narration. This is not a reason to remove purposeful manipulation. |
| L11 — Soni et al., *A Framework of Touchscreen Interaction Design Recommendations for Children (TIDRC)* (2019), [authors' paper](https://stirlab.org/wp-content/uploads/A-Framework-of-Touchscreen-Interaction-Design-Recommendations-for-Children-TIDRC-Characterizing-the-Gap-between-Research-Evidence-and-Design-Practice.pdf) | Child-interaction evidence synthesis and comparison with design practice supports clear visual/audio instructions, spacing, recognizable controls and feedback. | A design synthesis, not efficacy evidence. Its scope excluded disability-specific studies, so it cannot replace inclusive native testing. Use a quiet work surface, one action at a time and visible recovery. |
| L12 — Education Development Center, *Duolingo ABC* evaluation (January 2022), [full report](https://duolingo-papers.s3.amazonaws.com/reports/edc-report-on-duolingo-abc.pdf) | Nine-week study reported promising pre/post literacy changes. | Report explicitly lacks randomized treatment assignment and cannot establish causality; sample composition limits generalization. Do not repeat the marketed percentage as a causal reading gain or a target for this product. |

For evaluating this product over time, [Nesta's Standards of Evidence](https://www.nesta.org.uk/feature/innovation-methods/standards-evidence/)
provides a useful progression from a coherent theory of change to outcome evidence,
causal comparison and replication. That progression informs the staged plan below;
no Nesta level or certification has been awarded. The
[EEF digital technology guidance](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/digital)
also puts teaching and useful practice ahead of adopting technology for its own sake.

## How the teaching approaches translate

These are design decisions inferred from the evidence, not established effects of
Read to Lead. Keep human literacy review of the exact content/audio version.

| Approach | Use in this product | Avoid inferring |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Systematic phonics / explicit instruction | Demonstrate sound-spelling links; blend and segment; teach cumulatively and review; preserve supported versus independent attempts | One branded sequence is uniquely best; a correct tile match proves spoken reading |
| Language-rich and meaning-focused teaching | Explain workshop vocabulary orally; include brief reviewed connected text and optional conversation/retelling beyond the screen | Phonics alone is the entire reading curriculum, or narration alone is a language intervention |
| Montessori-inspired learning | Orderly material tray, clear demonstration, child choice among appropriate tasks, repetition without pressure, understandable consequences and self-correction | The app is a Montessori classroom, is certified, or inherits school-study effects; digital dragging is equivalent to physical manipulative work |
| Guided play / constructivist exploration | Give a specific reading objective, useful action, choice and authored support; allow experimentation in the mission payoff | An unguided child will discover the alphabetic code, or play has demonstrated literacy superiority |
| STEM inquiry | Predict an outcome, read an instruction, build/test, observe and describe; introduce technical vocabulary orally before using it in meaning tasks | A robot theme itself teaches science, engineering or executive function |
| Waldorf/Steiner-associated principles | Retain story, imagination, gentle pacing and respect for readiness where they fit | The limited observational timing evidence proves delayed teaching is better; delayed formal reading is the selected product direction |
| Whole-word familiarity / picture cues | Explicitly teach necessary irregular spellings and meanings within a cumulative plan | A picture-based guess or memorized game item establishes decoding; pictures should not disclose answers in independent probes |
| Songs, tracing, movement and multisensory presentation | Later use when they serve a defined reviewed objective and remain accessible | More senses or more animation necessarily improves learning; a preferred activity identifies a fixed learning style |

The proposed model is **teach → supported practice → independent opportunity →
meaningful use → revisit and transfer**. Rewards mark participation and workshop
progress. They must not convert assisted completion into a mastery score. Keep
five-to-ten-minute optional sessions as a product design starting point, not a
proven optimal dose or daily requirement.

### What would justify saying the experience is competitive

Use two adult reviewers on the same actual iPad and, separately, a Samsung Android
device. Record country/store, date, installed version, device/OS, orientation,
download state, accessibility settings and task. Compare starting an introductory
lesson, understanding a prompt, making two errors, finding help, using tap-only
controls, leaving/returning and reaching a natural end. Separate online and
airplane-mode conditions; unavailable content is unavailable evidence.

Record taps to first meaningful action, competing motion during instruction,
unavoidable waiting, replay discoverability, recovery, readable targets and exit
clarity. Score clarity, visual consistency, meaningful feedback, recovery,
accessibility and completion separately using written anchors: 1 = blocks the task,
3 = works with friction, 5 = clear and consistent throughout the observed task.
Two reviewers should record their observations independently and explain disagreement.
An average score cannot excuse an inaccessible core action.

Measure this app's frame/touch/audio performance with native instruments. Do not
infer competitors' internal metrics from a screenshot or award. A favorable matched
comparison can support a bounded usability/design conclusion, not "best-selling"
or "best educational app." Use the same process again when versions change.

## Learning and usability evaluation proposal

All child participation remains subject to the existing
[content review](content-review.md), [device acceptance](device-validation.md)
and [mobile readiness plan](mobile-readiness-plan.md). No recruitment, testing or
data collection was initiated. Participant-level data and consent records belong
in an approved private process, not Git or design/research connectors.

| Stage | Proposed procedure | What the result can establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adult validation | Literacy review of exact wording/recordings and cumulative sequence; native offline/accessibility/interaction checks | Content and operational suitability for the next gate; not child usability or learning effects |
| Small feasibility pilot | Planning assumption: 12–20 families over 2–3 weeks; optional short sessions, accessible participation and clear stop/withdrawal procedure | Navigation independence, understood prompts, support burden, frustration, natural endings and voluntary return; report counts and denominators, not causal gains |
| Exploratory learning/transfer | Assessor-led baseline/follow-up with fresh matched items; delayed follow-up around 2–4 weeks; paper or another non-game format as well as app stimuli | Early evidence of retained skill and transfer. Without a randomized comparator, maturation, usual teaching, practice and selection remain explanations |
| Efficacy study | Qualified independent evaluator; preregistered randomized comparison of app + usual teaching versus a credible time-matched alternative + usual teaching; allocation-blind assessors | Causal inference for the tested version/population if the design and execution support it; plan power, attrition, clustering and missing data before recruitment |
| Practical effectiveness and replication | Test normal home use with ordinary caregiver support, varied appropriate learners and devices; replicate promising results | Whether effects persist beyond researcher-supported conditions, who benefits, caregiver burden and what valuable activity is displaced |

Prespecify one primary independent reading outcome, baseline adjustment,
intention-to-treat analysis and confidence intervals for an efficacy study. Include
all assigned participants in the analysis strategy, not only enthusiastic completers.
Do not choose sample size by convenience or treat the pilot sample as powered.
A qualified researcher should justify a meaningful effect size and measurement
instrument; independent assessment rights and administration requirements matter.

Keep these observations separate:

- Trained-item success, new combinations of taught patterns, connected reading,
  oral vocabulary and comprehension are different outcomes. Carefully reviewed
  pseudowords may help a researcher examine decoding but are not required game content.
- In-app first-attempt success must distinguish prior models, replay, hints and
  motor errors. Prompt completion is not an observed spoken reading response.
- Navigation rescue, deliberate hint use and a physical misdrop are different
  events. Optional help is not a product failure or an undesirable behavior to suppress.
- Retention concerns later skill; voluntary return concerns experience. Screen
  time, streaks and repeated levels are not learning outcomes.
- Study the opportunity cost: shared reading, sleep, outdoor play and effective
  teaching should not be displaced merely to increase app usage.

The defensible current description is: **an adult development prototype designed
around evidence-informed beginning-reading principles**. Montessori equivalence,
STEM gains, validated mastery and this app's learning efficacy remain unproven.
